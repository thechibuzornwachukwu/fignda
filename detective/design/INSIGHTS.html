Lantern Row insights

The research behind the promise and the choices still ahead. Updated 12 Oct 2026. What has been built is in SPEC.md; what is left is in BUILD_PLAN.md.

People have not lost the ability to pay attention. They have lost the habit of staying with one thing. Lantern Row can honestly offer the opposite of a feed: a minute of slow, careful reading that ends, and then another if the player wants it.

The short version

  • It is a habit problem. Screen attention has shortened and reading for pleasure has fallen for 20 years. Nothing shows the brain itself has changed.
  • Promise the practice, never a result. Brain training claims failed in the lab and cost one company $2 million.
  • The mechanic already fits. Hidden words are only found by reading letter by letter, which is the one thing a feed never asks for.
  • A sitting is about a minute. A sentence, or 2 or 3, hiding 3 to 5 words. Short, but never thin.
  • Play first, then ask at the right moment. A wall before the first puzzle loses people. Never asking loses them later, quietly.
  • A sponsor buys reach, finishes and visits. Count everyone, guests included, and count the link.
  • Keep every game element. Change what each one rewards: skill and winnable contests, not showing up.

2. What we can and cannot say

A 2016 review of more than 130 brain training studies found that people get better at the trained game and little else. Lumosity paid $2 million over claims about school, work and memory. We can show a player getting better at Lantern Row. We cannot show it makes anyone smarter.

We can sayWe must not say
5 minutes of slow, careful reading a day.Fixes your attention span. Reverses brain rot.
A puzzle you finish. No feed, no autoplay.Improves memory, focus, grades or IQ.
You found a word only 8% found. Here are your records.Clinically proven. Scientists agree.
Every hidden word is checked by the engine."Written by a person" or "No slop", while any puzzle is machine-made.

3. Why this game fits

Fluent readers do not read letters. They predict the word and move on. Proofreading research shows it from the other side: errors in predictable words are the ones people miss, and people who look longer find more. A word hidden across "a most" is invisible to prediction and obvious to slow looking. The skill is looking, not knowing.

A feedLantern Row
LengthEndlessOne paragraph. It ends.
What starts the next thingAutoplayYou do, tomorrow
What it rewardsMoving on fastStaying and looking
How it endsYou give upDone for today

Daily puzzles that end are already a mass habit: Wordle was played 5.3 billion times in 2024.

4. The promise

Slow down. Look closer.

One paragraph a day with words hidden in plain sight. About 5 minutes, then it is over. No feed.

The dare stays as the hook: "Think you read carefully? You don't." The promise is what the player gets for taking it. It commits us to 3 things:

  1. It ends. After the daily the app says done and means it.
  2. It is worth reading. Every puzzle passes the same bar: the words are truly hidden, the sentences read naturally, nothing in it is rude or hateful.
  3. It respects you. No guilt in reminders. Nothing sold changes a score.

5. How long a sitting is

The owner's rule, 10 Oct 2026: a puzzle can be a sentence, or 2 or 3. In rare cases, longer. The evidence agrees, with one warning.

40 to 47 seconds

How long people stay on one screen before they switch (median 40, average 47). A sitting should end inside about a minute, with the first find in the first seconds.

Measured, in offices
21 to 41 seconds

The length of short videos that are watched to the end most, across several analyses. The shortest clips finish most often but hold people least. The middle wins.

Vendor analyses, they disagree
Shorter was not better

Duolingo tested shorter lessons to see if people would do more of them. They did not, and time spent learning fell.

Company's own account
About 6.7 minutes

Median session in the shortest genre of mobile games. A session is several sittings, so the next one must be one tap away.

Industry data, 2019

So: a sitting is 1 to 3 sentences that hide 3 to 5 words. Not fewer than 3: a sitting must end with something won. The 25 catalogue puzzles cut into 83 sittings this way, 81 of them 3 sentences or fewer. The whole puzzle stays as the hard one, and the daily is not cut.

Not proven: that this generation cannot attend for longer. The same people watch films and play long games. What changed is how quickly something has to earn the next minute.

6. Guests and accounts

What the evidence saysFor Lantern Row
Duolingo moved sign up back a few steps and reported about 20% more daily players. It then asked at later moments with walls that could be skipped.No wall before the first puzzle, ever. Ask more than once, later.
One game that let people play first saw far fewer leave, and fewer sign ups. Many of the forced accounts had been worthless.Count players who return, not accounts made.
A guest cannot be reached: no email, no reminder. One publisher reported 4.5 times the day 7 return for signed in players.A guest who forgets us is gone. The account is how we bring them back.
Wordle players lost their runs when they changed phone or cleared their browser, and it made the news.Say plainly that a guest's run lives in one browser.
Safari clears a site's stored data after 7 days without a visit, in a normal tab.A guest's run, stars, records and kept games can vanish. Not checked against Safari's current rules.

Recommended, not built:

  1. Turn on Google sign in first. A 6 digit email code is the hardest step in the flow. One tap removes it.
  2. After a guest's first daily, show their place: "You would be 14th of 60 today." A real rank is a reason. "Keep this score" is not.
  3. At a run of 3 days, say the run lives in this browser only and sign in keeps it anywhere. Information, not guilt.
  4. Everything else stays open. No play limits and no progress taken away. Nothing locks a player out.

7. Six principles for the game

From the owner's notes on game mechanics, checked against research. No element is removed: score, points, all 13 badges, boards, streaks, friend streaks, rooms and avatars stay. Each change says what the element should reward.

PrincipleEvidenceLantern Row todayChange
1. The scoreboard is not the gameRewards for something people already enjoy can lower interest in it. Gamified learning shows a small gain across 35 studies.A good core with a lot stacked on it.Work on the find: a reveal of where each missed word hid, harder finds across 2 or 3 joins, a "clean read".
2. Local and winnableLow ranks on a big board lower motivation. Strava's Local Legends gives ordinary people a title they can win.A global board. Circles sit behind a link.Your circle's board first. A crown for the first in your circle to find each word. Weekly tables of about 20.
3. Less at oncePast a point each feature costs more attention than it returns.A new player can meet a dozen features in one session.Each element appears when the player has done what it scores. New features replace old ones.
4. Anticipation, not obligationStreaks run on loss aversion. The EU's coming Digital Fairness Act looks at streaks that punish a break. Uncertain rewards hold attention more than fixed ones.The daily hides its count, which is the best anticipation in the game. A missed day resets the run.1 free rest day a week, never sold. Some days hide a long or deep word. Reminders talk about the puzzle.
5. The drive to completeEffort rises near a goal, and visible progress makes people finish. Apple's rings close an open shape.Packs show "12 / 30". The daily shows no progress.A ring that closes. The daily says "3 left" near the end. A week ring.
6. Skill over attendanceFeeling competent predicts coming back more than rank does. Peloton's records mark real improvement.11 of 13 badges are for streak length or lifetime points.A skill family of badges shown first, personal records, and a Sharp eye figure.

8. What to measure

  • Players who come back 7 days after their first daily.
  • Games played to the end out of games started, per puzzle. Counted for everyone since 10 Oct 2026.
  • Guests who become accounts: new profiles a day against games started a day. This is the number any change to guest mode must move.
  • Sittings in one visit. If short sittings work, this rises and time on the puzzle does not fall.
  • Median wrong picks per daily over a player's first 14 days. If the promise is true, this falls.

9. How a detective works a case

The game says the player is a detective, so a chapter should be worked the way a case is. This section is the research behind that, and what each finding changes. It is a proposal: none of it is built. The screens are in the prototype canvas "Lantern Row case storyline".

What detectives doWhere it comes fromLantern Row todayChange
1. Scene, people, records, in that orderPolice procedure: the first job is to protect the scene and record it, then separate and canvass witnesses, then follow up with records and interviews.A chapter is word clue, word clue, number clue, in the same order every time, set nowhere.A chapter opens on a brief, then the scene, then statements, then records. Each step is set in a place.
2. Every step leaves a leadProcedure again: each interview and each record is followed up. Nothing is done for its own sake.A clue ends on stars and a letter. The next clue has nothing to do with it.Each step ends on a fact that names where to go next: a ladder seen, so the key log; a key not returned, so the store room.
3. Rule people outStudies of failed investigations: tunnel vision is fixing on one suspect early and reading every fact as support. Trained investigators showed it more than lay people in one study. The cure is to weigh every plausible account.Clues spell out a hidden item, then the last clue spells out a culprit nobody had heard of.All suspects are on a board from the brief. Evidence clears them, one at a time. It never points at anybody until the line-up.
4. Means, motive and chanceCasework builds motive from records and from who was close to what was taken. Not sourced here: the 3-part test itself is common knowledge, and no source for it was read.The confession gives a reason at the end. Nothing before it does.3 steps of a chapter answer how, why and when. The line-up checks all 3 fit before a name is given.
5. The culprit is met early, and every clue is shownKnox's rules for detective stories, 1929: the criminal must be introduced early, and the detective may not use a clue the reader was not shown. Written as a fair fight between writer and reader.The culprit appears for the first time at the unmasking.Nobody new at the end. The ending lists the evidence step by step, so the answer can be checked.
6. Chapters differThe stories do: a closed circle of suspects (Christie), a culprit known from the start (Columbo), a ghost that is a costume (Scooby-Doo), a theft that is not one (Holmes). Not sourced: general knowledge of those stories.Every chapter has the same games in the same order.5 shapes: who did it, prove it, what is it really, where is it, nothing was taken. A shape picks 5 or 6 methods and its own ending.
7. More ways of lookingEvidence comes in kinds: a room, what people say, what was written down, things torn or left behind.4 methods: hidden words, matching numbers, reading backwards, spelling a name.New methods by kind of evidence: spot what is off (a scene), fit the pieces (a torn note), 4 pictures and 1 word (a link), the line-up. Then ink not word, bigger tag, odd one out, match the print, put it in order.
8. Nobody works aloneThe owner's note of 12 Oct 2026.A hint, and a room for a whole puzzle."Call for backup" on any step: a tip-off from the pet (the hint, renamed), a friend called into the step, or a favour called in.

Real cases: the trail the suspect never saw

No suspect leaves clues for the detective. In the cases below the trail was something the suspect did not know existed, did not think mattered, or believed they had destroyed. Each one is a pattern a chapter can use. Only the method is borrowed: the game's cases stay harmless, so nothing from a violent case is retold.

CaseWhat gave them awayThe patternThe game it suggests
Antwerp diamond heist, 2003The gang dumped their rubbish by a road. A man who looked after the land found it the next morning. In it: a receipt in the ringleader's name, and a half-eaten sandwich that gave DNA. A second receipt led police to a shop's camera footage. He was the prime suspect before he walked back in a week later to look innocent.What they threw away. A receipt is a name, a place and a time.Fit the pieces: a torn receipt put back in order. Then check its time against the other records.
Wolfgang Beltracchi, art forger, caught 2010He tested his paints so no modern pigment would show. One tube of white, bought ready made, held traces of titanium white, which is not found in paintings until the 1940s. His picture was dated 1914. A scientist found the trace in a sketch on the back.One thing that could not have been there yet. Care everywhere, and a slip in the place nobody looks.Odd one out, and "ink, not word": the label says one thing and the material says another.
The Great Train Robbery, 1963The gang hid at a farm and wiped it down before leaving. Prints survived on a Monopoly board they had played on with the money, and on a ketchup bottle. A neighbour's suspicion sent police to the farm.The trace left while relaxing. People clean the tools and forget the game.Match the print: one mark against several that are nearly the same.
The Lindbergh ransom, 1932 to 1934 (method only)The ransom was paid in notes whose serial numbers had been written down first. Over 2 years they surfaced one at a time. A petrol station attendant, wary of an unusual note, wrote the car's number plate in its margin. A bank teller traced it.A list made before anything was spent, and a record kept by a stranger for his own reasons.Matching numbers: find the serial numbers from the list among the notes in a till.

What the 4 share. The suspect was careful about the thing itself and careless about what surrounded it: the rubbish, a tube of paint, a board game, the change from a purchase. And in 3 of the 4 the break came from an ordinary person doing an ordinary job, not from the detective. So a chapter can give a step to a caretaker, a shopkeeper or a bank clerk.

The rule this gives the game is in BUILD_PLAN.md, Direction: the 4 kinds of clue. What the cases add to it: a torn letter answers why, and who else knew. A torn receipt answers what was bought and when. A transfer slip answers who paid whom. Hidden words have a reason under this rule only when the text is a message passed in plain sight, never when it is a witness talking. The game already has a figure who teaches that: the one behind the card with the open eye. So each case's hidden-words step can be a message to that figure, and the messages add up across cases.

Methods the cases and the labs suggest. Document examiners recover writing from the sheets under the one written on, by light from the side or by an electrostatic machine, 3 or 4 pages down. A carbon film typewriter ribbon holds every letter that was typed, in a row. Both are a reason for a method the game has or wants: rubbing a pad to bring up what was written above it, and finding words in a run of letters with no spaces. The points on blotters, lemon juice and which way glass falls are common knowledge and were not sourced.

What follows from it. The sets to build and the decisions still open are in BUILD_PLAN.md, section 1 and the owner's list. One thing belongs here because it is about the promise: none of this is a claim about memory, focus or attention. The colour methods come from a test psychologists use, and that must never be said in the game.

Writing a chapter: what weak stories do, and what ours does

A case is only as good as its story. On 12 Oct 2026 we read what readers, editors and one large study say about weak mysteries and about stories people take for machine-made, then wrote the first chapter, The long spoon, against that list. The rules it gave are in BUILD_PLAN.md, Direction, and checkChapter in src/engine/chapter.ts refuses a chapter that breaks the ones a program can check.

What weak stories doWhere it comes fromWhat a chapter does
Tidy, on 1 track, and it explains its own pointA study of more than 50,000 machine-written short stories (University of Maryland and Google DeepMind, a preprint, not yet reviewed): the tell is how the story is built, more than its words. Plots are neat and single, themes are spelled out, and no choice is hard.The ending says what was found, what the culprit says and what is still undecided. It draws no lesson. In The long spoon nobody ever learns whether the name was planed off fairly.
The culprit has no real reasonReaders on mysteries they gave up on: a culprit with no motive spoils the book.The motive is human and comes from the past of the place: a name removed 15 years ago. It is mischief, pride or a surprise, never harm.
A clue is kept back, or the answer comes by luckKnox's rules, 1929, and Van Dine's: the reader must have every clue the detective uses, and the answer must come from reasoning, never from accident, coincidence or a confession nobody earned.Anything the ending relies on is in the brief or a step first. The smell of burnt wood is in the brief. The 9 finals are in the brief and again in the message.
Clues are decorationA screenwriting note: clues that are not used make the ending feel unearned. Build the mystery backward from what the culprit had to do.A chapter is plotted backward from the culprit's evening: what they needed, what they touched, what they tried to destroy, what they forgot. Every clue is used.
Everybody tells the truthThe rules of a fair mystery allow suspects to lie and allow false trails. A cast that only tells the truth has no texture.A suspect may lie, for a small reason of their own, and a record then shows it. The champion says she was home: she was selling at a stall with no permit.
The detective explains it all at the endThe same screenwriting note: a long telling to people who already know what happened.The line-up shows means, motive and chance in 3 short lines. The player names who is left.
The words give it awayLists of machine habits in prose: "quietly", "not this but that", stock names, a flat voice with no change of pace.The copy rules already forbid most of it. Names and places are from home. A line is 240 characters at most.

One caution from the same reading: no single word or mark proves a text was written by a machine. People used every one of them first. So the list above is about how a story is built, which is also what makes it good.

10. How the cases are structured

The game is cases now, so the question is how many, in what order, how long, and what brings a player back to the next one. On 13 Oct 2026 we read how Duolingo lays out a course and how 6 games built on mysteries or on teaching a skill lay out theirs. This section is what they do, what we take, and what we leave. The structure it gives is in BUILD_PLAN.md, "How the cases are structured".

What they doWhere it comes fromWhat we take
1 path, and nothing optional beside itDuolingo, 2022. The old home screen was a tree: each skill had 5 levels, only the first was required, and by its own account many learners rarely went further. The new one is a single path. Each skill is 3 levels set on the path in order, with practice and Stories on the path too, so nobody has to choose what to do next.Cases sit on 1 path and the Play button always opens the next thing. Practice and the short interludes are on the path, not in a menu. A method has 3 levels, not 5.
New and old are mixed, and what is new comes back soonDuolingo says its path follows spaced repetition: new material is met again soon, then at longer gaps, and a lesson mixes new ideas with old ones due for review. Classroom studies of mixed practice (Rohrer and Taylor) find it feels harder while practising and holds better later, and that a learner needs a little practice of 1 thing alone first.A case brings in 1 new method at most. It is met alone and easy, then again in each of the next 2 cases mixed with older ones. No case is all 1 method.
A named group of units, with a bannerDuolingo groups units into named sections. Criminal Case groups cases into districts of a city, about 5 or 6 to a district, with a storyline the cases of a district share, and a new city each season.Cases are grouped into a casebook: 8 cases in 1 place, with its own name and look. A new casebook is a new place.
Every case has the same bonesCriminal Case: a cutscene, crime scenes to search, suspects to question, evidence to the lab, then name the culprit from 5 pieces of evidence. Ace Attorney: each episode alternates investigation and trial, and the next part opens when enough evidence is in. Sherlock Holmes Consulting Detective: a case booklet, leads followed in any order, then questions.Every case is brief, steps, line-up, ending. What changes is the shape, the methods and the story, never the bones. A step opens when the one before is done.
Guessing is made pointless, not punishedReturn of the Obra Dinn confirms answers only 3 at a time, so a correct guess cannot be told from a wrong one until 3 are right. Criminal Case charges a star to name a culprit, and a wrong name costs it.Whoever the evidence cleared cannot be named, and the line-up has 2 tries. A player who earned every clearing has 1 face left: the work is what makes the answer plain.
Efficiency is scored against a masterSherlock Holmes Consulting Detective scores a case against Holmes: points for right answers, and 5 points off for each lead followed beyond the number he needed.Stars already do this: 3 for a step read clean with no tip-off. We do not add a second score.
A case has an afterword that opens the nextCriminal Case will not open the next case until the solved one has had a further chapter: follow-ups with the people who were cleared.The ending says what is still undecided. 1 loose end of each case is the first line of the next brief.
A level teaches 1 idea in 4 beatsNintendo, as its designer Koichi Hayashida has described it and others have analysed it: introduce an idea where failing costs nothing, develop it, twist it, then conclude with a test, all in about 5 minutes, after a 4 part form used in East Asian verse and comics.Inside a case: a method is met plain, then harder, then with a twist (a suspect who lied, the obvious one cleared), then the line-up tests the whole case.
Standalone stories, with a longer thread behindTelevision procedurals sit between pure case of the week and pure serial. The X-Files mixed standalone episodes with a running conspiracy, and its standalone episodes are the ones many viewers liked best.Most cases stand alone. 2 in each casebook of 8 carry the Open Eye: the 4th and the last. A player who skips a week loses nothing.
New story every week, at a known costJune's Journey: a chapter is 5 scenes and a puzzle scene, a new chapter comes out each week, and its maker has said 1 scene takes about 30 hours to produce.A case is cheaper to make than a drawn scene, but not free. We plan 1 case a fortnight and say so, and a season's special case sits beside the path, never in its way.
A story is its puzzles, or the puzzles are fillerProfessor Layton: the first game was criticised for puzzles that had nothing to do with the plot ("people around here just like puzzles"). Later games tied more of them to the story and were praised for it.Every method has a physical reason, and a step exists because the case needs its fact. A step that could be lifted out without the case changing is cut.

One thing we expected to use and will not. The idea that people remember an unfinished task better than a finished one (the Zeigarnik effect, 1927) is the usual argument for ending on a cliffhanger. Later studies in other countries did not find it, and a 2025 review of the research found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks. What the same review did find is a general tendency to go back and finish a task that was interrupted (the Ovsiankina effect). So we do not count on a player remembering where they were. We end a sitting on a step with a lead, which is something to go back to, and the board shows what was found so nothing has to be remembered.

What was decided from it, 13 Oct 2026

DecisionWhat we choseWhy
The placeLantern Row: a market street with a hall, a bakery, a bus stop and a library. Casebook 1 takes its name, and all 8 cases happen there.Criminal Case keeps a district's cases in 1 place with people who return, and that is what makes its cases feel like 1 story. A street that is nowhere in particular can be anybody's, which a game for players anywhere needs.
The castThe 14 characters the game can draw live on the street. Somebody cleared in 1 case can be a witness in the next. Nobody is the culprit twice in a casebook.Returning people are what a district has that a list of puzzles does not. It also costs nothing: no new faces need drawing.
The 8 casesSuspects run 4, 2, 1, 3, 4, 2, 1, 5. All 5 shapes are used before any repeats twice. 1 new method a case, none in the last.Duolingo mixes new with old and never asks for 2 new things at once. The last case tests everything, as the last beat of a Nintendo level does.
The threadThe first case leaves it open whether a name was removed fairly in 2011. Case 4 finds the record and case 8 reads it. The Open Eye is behind it and is never named.A procedural with a thread gives a reason to reach the end, and standalone cases mean nobody is lost by missing 1. 2 thread cases in 8 is the lighter end of that mix, on purpose.
The paceThe player's own. Every written case is open in order and nothing waits for a date: no timer, no daily cap, no wait between cases. We write about 1 a fortnight and plan for a player 3 times faster than we expect. Casebook 1 becomes the game only when 4 of its 8 cases are written. At the end of what is written there are cold cases, practice, a seasonal case when one is open, and the next file with "Tell me when it opens".The first version of this decision released 1 case a fortnight, and the owner caught the fault: a player who finishes in 3 days would be locked out by our writing speed. Duolingo's path is all there from the start and a learner sets the pace (general knowledge of the app, not sourced). June's Journey ships weekly because its content is costly, not because waiting is good for players. Our own rule already says nothing expires and nothing locks a player out.
ArtCase 5 needs the first drawn room. If it is late, case 6 takes its slot. Both are written to stand in either.The one thing here we do not control is how long a drawing takes. A path that waits on art is a path that stops.

How sure we are. Duolingo's account of its own path is from its blog. Criminal Case and June's Journey are from fan wikis, a help page and player posts, and the counts differ between them. The Nintendo method is from secondary accounts of interviews. The classroom numbers on mixed practice vary between the summaries read, so no figure is quoted here. None of this says a structure will hold players: it says what people who had to find out by running a game ended up doing.
